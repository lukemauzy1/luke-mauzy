After a physical setback during first assembly pulled solder pads off the original board, the project was redesigned from scratch, and the scope grew. Revision 2 is a two-layer custom PCB around the Raspberry Pi Pico built as a **handheld game console**, not a single game: it adds battery-powered operation with a **BQ24075 Li-ion charger**, a **vibration motor** for haptic feedback, a **slide power switch**, and **three additional buttons**, and the software story is now a **multi-game launcher** that loads games from a microSD card. The board runs an integrated schematic-to-fabrication workflow: hardware selection, pin mapping, schematic capture, custom footprint creation, layout, grounding strategy, and fab-file preparation.

## Objectives

- Integrate multiple input/output peripherals into a single embedded hardware platform
- Add self-contained operation through battery charging and power management
- Turn the build into a handheld **multi-game console** rather than a single Pong game
- Load and manage a library of games from a microSD card with a clean launcher UI
- Create a manufacturable custom PCB rather than relying on breadboard wiring
- Redesign the board to correct the issues that caused the first assembly to fail
- Validate the design through electrical rule checks and PCB design rule checks

## System Architecture

The PCB is built around the Raspberry Pi Pico as the main microcontroller platform. Revision 2 peripherals include:

- 5-pin analog joystick module (with center push switch)
- 3.2" 18-pin SPI TFT display module (**ILI9341**) with touchscreen functionality (**XPT2046**) and an onboard SD card slot
- 32 GB microSD card (via a regular SD adapter) used with the display's SD slot
- 4 tactile pushbuttons
- Piezo buzzer driven through an N-MOSFET
- Coin-form vibration motor for haptic feedback, driven through an N-MOSFET
- 3.7 V Li-ion cell with a **BQ24075RGT** single-cell charging IC
- SPDT slide switch for power on/off
- Polyfuse overcurrent protection and decoupling capacitors

The design uses the Pico's 3.3 V regulated rail to power the logic and low-current peripherals, while the joystick's analog outputs route to the Pico's ADC pins, the display is interfaced over SPI, and the new buttons join the joystick switch as digital GPIO inputs. The motor's MOSFET switch keeps the higher-current haptic load isolated from the logic supply. The display's SPI bus also carries its touch (XPT2046) and SD-card lines, and while the charging circuitry sits on the front of the board, the Li-ion battery itself is mounted on the back.

## Game Library and Launcher

The software goal is a small handheld console: the microSD card holds a library of games, and the device boots into a launcher that enumerates them through a filepath structure rather than hard-coding a single title. The planned layout:

- `/games/` at the SD root, with one folder per game (e.g. `/games/pong/`, `/games/snake/`, `/games/breakout/`)
- each game folder carries a small metadata file (title, author, and version) plus its assets and code
- the launcher scans `/games/`, renders the titles as a menu on the ILI9341, and lets the joystick/buttons navigate and confirm a selection
- Pong ships first as the flagship title, with the menu/game state machine and AI from the original firmware refactored into this framework

Keeping games as separate SD folders means new titles can be dropped onto the card without reflashing the firmware.

## Key Technical Work

### 1. Hardware Interface Definition

Mapped and validated signal assignments between the Pico and every external module:

- analog joystick axes to ADC-capable GPIOs
- joystick switch and all four tactile buttons to digital GPIO inputs
- SPI display clock, data, chip select, reset, and data/command lines to appropriate GPIO pins, with the display's touch (XPT2046) and SD-card lines riding alongside the same SPI bus
- buzzer and vibration motor PWM outputs routed to MOSFET gate drivers, with a flyback diode across the motor to clamp the inductive spike when Q2 switches off
- charging and power-management signals from the BQ24075

Because the Pico sits on the back while everything else lives on the front, most SPI signals route through vias. Signal integrity is handled in code rather than with added hardware: GPIO drive strength is kept at 2-4 mA (default rather than boosting to 8/12 mA) to soften edge rates and avoid ringing across via jumps, the SPI clock stays at or below 25 MHz, and traces take the shortest direct path between Pico pins and each peripheral.

A key design decision was distinguishing between SPI TFT labeling and I2C conventions, since the display's SCL and SDA pins represented SPI clock and MOSI, not I2C lines.

### 2. Battery Charging and Power Management

Added a self-contained power path around the **BQ24075RGT**, a linear single-cell Li-ion/Li-Poly charger with system power-path management:

- 500 mAh 3.7 V cell with a polyfuse (750 mA hold) for overcurrent protection
- ISET resistor (R2 = 3.9 kΩ) sets the charge current: 890 A·Ω ÷ 3900 Ω ≈ **228 mA**, so a full charge takes ~2.19 hours
- ILIM resistor (R4 = 2.7 kΩ) caps USB input current at 1550 A·Ω ÷ 2700 Ω ≈ **574 mA**
- D1 series diode in the power path
- system rail provides power to the board logic, so the device can charge and run from a single cell
- filled copper zones taper the wide power traces down to the narrower BQ24075 pins, since KiCad has no built-in way to step trace widths without a plugin

The charger section was placed and routed with thermal relief in mind, since linear charging can dissipate noticeable heat at the IC. A right-angle slider switch (**MSS 3111**, rated 1 A AC / 3 A DC) sits in series between the BQ24075 OUT pin and Vsys, able to poke out the top of the enclosure and comfortably carry the board's 300-400 mA draw.

The system's worst-case current is about **240 mA** with everything active at once (RP2040 + display/backlight + busy SD card + touch/joystick + motor + buzzer), which stays under the Pico's 3.3 V regulator limit of 300 mA.

### 3. Schematic Capture and Electrical Validation

Built the full Revision 2 schematic in KiCad and resolved issues including:

- unused and intentionally unconnected pins
- power net naming consistency across the new battery and charging nets
- correct pull-up logic for button inputs
- proper distribution of the 3.3 V rail and the battery/charging rail

Electrical Rule Check (ERC) was used to identify and resolve connection and net-labeling issues before layout.

### 4. Custom Footprint Development

Developed and updated custom PCB footprints for the non-standard modules based on measured pin geometry, including the joystick, the TFT display, and the slide switch (the vibration motor rode along on its datasheet footprint):

- defining pad sizes and drill diameters
- setting correct pad pitch
- marking pin 1 orientation clearly
- verifying footprint pad numbering against schematic symbol pin numbering

This remained a critical part of the project because module-level hardware often lacks standardized footprints.

### 5. PCB Layout and Layer Planning

Designed a two-layer PCB in KiCad. The board grew significantly for Revision 2 to fit the added power-management and haptic components, measuring roughly 19.5 cm × 9.1 cm, with:

- Raspberry Pi Pico placed on the back side
- battery charging circuitry (BQ24075, polyfuse, and support parts) placed on the front side, with the Li-ion battery itself attaching to the back
- coin vibration motor placed in the blank area at the top left of the front side
- display, joystick, buttons, and switch placed on the front side

The layout keeps the higher-current charger and motor paths separate from sensitive analog and SPI routing, and because the Pico is on the back, every front-side signal that talks to it jumps through vias; the design ended up via-heavy by necessity. Mechanical considerations include display window alignment, joystick access clearance, button accessibility, switch placement, and USB keep-out for the Pico.

### 6. Power and Grounding Strategy

Implemented a practical power-distribution strategy supporting both the logic load and the battery/charging path:

- power traces sized for the charging and motor currents where needed
- signal routing kept small
- front and back copper pours for the ground network

A key lesson from the layout phase was that shared net names alone do not guarantee physical connectivity between copper zones; plated through connections such as vias or through-hole pads are required to join front and back ground zones.

### 7. Design Rule Check Resolution

Ran PCB Design Rule Check (DRC) on the new layout and resolved issues spanning unconnected track stubs, board-edge clearance violations, silkscreen overlap with the board edge, thermal relief connections, and connectivity between the front and back ground zones. Working through each category improved both manufacturability and confidence in the redesigned board.

## Tools and Methods

- KiCad for schematic capture, footprint creation, PCB layout, ERC/DRC, and fabrication outputs
- Manual pinout verification and footprint measurement
- Gerber and drill file generation for manufacturing preparation

## Challenges and Lessons Learned

Bringing the original board up in hardware surfaced problems that never show up in simulation or layout review. During assembly, excessive heat, pressure, and repeated rework pulled solder pads off the board, lifting copper from the substrate and damaging the underlying traces. That failure forced a restart on a fresh board and directly drove the Revision 2 redesign: lower iron temperatures, less physical pressure, and avoiding reheating the same pad. It also highlighted the value of ordering spare components ahead of assembly rather than treating a single unit as sufficient for a first physical build.

## What's Left to Do

- **Soldering**: re-populate the Revision 2 board using the revised technique (lower iron temperature, shorter dwell time) to avoid pad lift
- **Firmware**: extend the MicroPython firmware to the console model: boot into a launcher that scans `/games/` on the SD card, render a scrollable game menu on the ILI9341, and hand control to the selected game. Pong becomes the first title, refactoring the existing menu/game/AI state machine and porting rendering from the OLED prototype to the 3.2" display over the via-heavy SPI bus
- **Game library**: define the `/games/` file structure and per-game metadata (title, author, version), then verify the launcher loads a second game end to end
- **Enclosure CAD**: design and model a 3D-printable enclosure accounting for the display window, joystick clearance, button and switch placement, the vibration motor, and the slide switch poking out the top

## Outcome

Produced a fully redesigned, near-fabrication-ready custom PCB that moves the build from breadboard to a manufactured platform, integrating input, analog sensing, display output, battery charging, and haptic feedback into one board, now positioned as the hardware for a palmtop game console with an SD-backed game library. The revision demonstrates embedded hardware design, power management, schematic capture, custom footprint generation, PCB layout, grounding and power distribution, signal-integrity-aware routing, and iterative ERC/DRC debugging.
