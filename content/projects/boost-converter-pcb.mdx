## Overview

This project involved the full design cycle of a DC-DC boost converter (also known as a step-up converter) capable of boosting a 5V input up to at least 12V across a specified load. The project spanned analytical calculations, KiCad simulation, PCB layout, physical assembly, and experimental verification.

Key concepts applied include time-dependent behavior of reactive components, pulse-width modulation (PWM) generation using a 555 timer, and MOSFET switching characteristics.

## How It Works

A boost converter stores energy in an inductor while a MOSFET switch is closed, then releases that energy to the output when the switch opens. By controlling the duty cycle of the PWM signal driving the MOSFET gate, the output voltage can be stepped up well above the input.

The PWM signal was generated using a 555 timer configured as an astable multivibrator, with a target duty cycle in the 50-90% range at 500 Hz.

## Key Technical Work

### 1. Component Value Calculations

**Timing resistors for the 555 timer (astable at 500 Hz):**

The duty cycle is bounded by:

- Lower bound: D = 0.5 (Ra = 0, 0.5 = Rb/2Rb and Rb cancels)
- Upper bound: D = 0.9, which gives Rb 1250 ohms

Closest kit value: R3 = 1200 ohms

**Timing capacitor C2:**

Using the astable period formula with f = 500 Hz:

- Case 1 (Ra = 0): C = 1.2 uF
- Case 2 (Ra = 10k): C = 0.23 uF

Closest kit value in range: C2 = 0.47 uF

**Timing resistor RA:**

Using the selected R3 = 1200 Ω and C2 = 0.47 µF to solve for RA to hit the 500 Hz target:

RA = 6,140.4 − 2,400 = 3,740 Ω

**Output filter capacitor C3:**

To maintain the output within 1% of 12V during the off-time, the minimum capacitance was calculated to be 60.33 uF.

Closest available value in kit: C3 = 100 uF

### 2. Inductor Energy Verification

The inductor's internal resistance was first measured directly (RL = 53 Ω) rather than assumed ideal. That measured value fed into a KVL loop equation combining inductor voltage, MOSFET drain-source voltage, and internal resistance, which was solved as a differential equation for inductor current over the on-time to get a real, non-idealized current profile rather than a simplified estimate.

Integrating that current profile gave an energy stored during the on-time of 113.6 µJ, exceeding the 108 µJ required by the load over one switching period and confirming the inductor selection was valid even after accounting for real winding resistance.

### 3. KiCad Simulation

Two transient simulations were run in KiCad using SPICE components:

- **PWM verification:** Confirmed a stable square wave at approximately 85% duty cycle, within the 50-90% target range
- **Output voltage verification:** Simulated output of 11.9V, within 1% of the 12V target

### 4. Experimental Results

After PCB fabrication and assembly, the board was tested with 5V and -5V supplies:

- **Measured duty cycle:** 80.4%, within the 50-90% design range
- **Measured output voltage:** approximately 12V, within 1% of target

Small discrepancies between analytical, simulated, and experimental values are attributed to PCB trace resistance, component tolerances, and non-ideal 555 timer behavior.

## Outcome

As a team, delivered a manufactured and tested boost converter PCB that met its 12V design target within 1% error, with measured hardware results tracking closely to both hand calculations and SPICE simulation.
